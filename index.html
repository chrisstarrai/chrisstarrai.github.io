01

One agent you trust beats ten you manage.

With a fleet of bots, you’re the orchestrator. With one agent, the agent is.

Read the essay

The pitch for AI agents right now is a team. A research bot, a sales bot, a coding bot, a recruiting bot, each with its own scope, its own tools and its own little dashboard. It looks like an org chart. I think that’s exactly the problem.

Roles are dissolving, and bots freeze them

Work doesn’t sort into clean roles anymore. In a single afternoon I might look at a hiring loop, fix a bug, prep for a partner meeting and get someone’s first week set up. AI made that possible, and it’s making it normal.

Scoped bots take the old job descriptions and turn them into software, right as those job descriptions stop meaning much. You end up with a tool shaped like the company you used to be.

Every bot is a relationship you have to build

Trust doesn’t transfer between agents. With each one you learn what it’s good at, where it fails and what it’s allowed to touch. That takes time, and you pay it again for every bot.

Context doesn’t transfer either. You explain the project to the sales bot, then again to the research bot, then again to the one that writes the follow-up. Ten bots means ten onboardings.

And then you’re their manager. You track which one is doing what, chase the one that got stuck, and reconcile answers that don’t agree. The work was supposed to come off your plate. Instead you got a team to run.

One agent, trusted once

The alternative is one agent you talk to in one thread. You build trust with it once, and it deepens with every task. Context compounds: it already knows the project, the people and how you like things done. When the work needs splitting, it splits it, keeps track of the pieces and comes back with the result.

That’s how I work with Arion, the agent Arena runs on. I keep talking in the same conversation, and it works out what becomes its own piece of work.

Underneath, it can look the same

To be fair to the bots: inside, a single agent may start workers, run specialised steps and do things in parallel. Architecturally, that can look a lot like a fleet.

The difference isn’t the machinery. It’s who manages it. With a fleet of bots, you’re the orchestrator. With one agent, the agent is. You hold one relationship and get the leverage of many.

What it looked like this week

I wanted a personal site. In one conversation, a lot of it from my phone, I asked for it, rejected the first version, asked for a dark mode switch, said publish it, then said set up email too. It published the site, pointed the domain and set up the mailbox. When I said cost mattered more to me than Gmail, it switched providers mid-task.

I never opened another tool or briefed another bot. I signed in where it needed me and said yes before it spent money. Everything else, it carried.

The tradeoff

One agent is a bigger bet. If it holds your trust across everything, it also holds more of your world, so a mistake reaches further.

That’s why I think security and design are the same problem here. The agent acts with your authority but never sees your passwords. It asks before the steps you can’t take back, and moves freely everywhere else. A single agent only works if it earns more trust than any one bot would ever need.

What’s still hard

Knowing when to split work without being told. Showing what’s happening underneath without turning back into a dashboard. The thread has to stay simple on the surface while the work behind it gets bigger and runs longer on its own. That’s the part I’m working on now.

I don’t want to manage agents. I want one I can trust.

02

Your agent shouldn’t need a computer.

Your phone, your watch, even a toaster. The device is just the interface. The computer lives in the cloud.

Read the essay

Most AI agents still assume you’re sitting at a computer. The agent runs on your laptop, in a terminal or a desktop app, and when the lid closes, the work stops. I think that’s backwards.

The device is just the interface

You should be able to talk to your agent from anywhere: your phone, your watch, the car, a toaster if it had a microphone. The thing in your hand only has to hear you and show you the result. The work happens somewhere else.

Once you see it that way, the device stops mattering. It doesn’t need to be powerful. It doesn’t need to be yours. It just needs to be there when you want to say something.

One computer was never enough

A laptop is one machine, with one battery, one network connection and one set of windows. Real work doesn’t fit in that. A big change wants a machine to build and test on, another to check it in a real browser, another to research the next thing while the first is still running.

In the cloud, that’s a few more machines. On a laptop, it’s a fan spinning and a lid you can’t close. Cloud machines keep going when you walk away, and they’re still there when you come back.

Coding from a phone

This is the part people push back on most. I think coding should be possible from a phone, with the laptop optional. Not typing code with your thumbs. Saying what you want, watching it get built and tested on machines you’ll never see, looking at the result, and saying ship it.

When the agent does the typing, the keyboard stops being the bottleneck. What’s left is judgement, and judgement works fine on a small screen.

What it looks like for me

I stopped using a desktop agent this year. Not because it was bad, but because I kept reaching for my phone instead, and Arion, the agent Arena runs on, just kept working there. It feels seamless.

Going back to a tool that only works when I’m at my desk now feels like going back to a phone with a cord.

The tradeoff

When the computer lives in the cloud, so does your work. That’s a real cost, and it’s why I think security has to come first here. The agent should act with your authority without ever holding your passwords, and its machines should carry as little as they possibly can.

What’s still hard

Signing in. A passkey on your phone doesn’t always reach a screen in the cloud, and every sign-in wall pulls you back toward a keyboard. Reviewing a big change on a small screen. Making something that runs far away feel as immediate as something on your desk.

None of that changes the direction. The future is minimal. The device is irrelevant.

It’s just the interface.

03

The best agent interface is the one you forget.

No modes, no model menus, no dashboard. You talk, and it comes back done.

Read the essay

Every agent product I try keeps adding things. A mode picker. A model menu. A panel for memory, a tab for every running task, a settings page that scrolls. Each one makes sense on its own. Together they turn the person into the operator of the thing they came to for help.

I think the best agent interface is the one you forget is there.

Relief is the feature

When an agent really works, the feeling isn’t power. It’s relief. You say the thing you’ve been carrying around, and it’s off your plate. You didn’t choose a mode or a model or a window. You talked, and it came back done.

That feeling is the product. Everything on the screen either protects it or gets in its way.

Hide it until it’s asked for

Complexity isn’t the enemy. Showing it too early is. A dropdown is the product asking you to decide something: which model, how hard to think, which agent handles this. Sometimes you care. Most of the time the honest answer is “whatever’s best, you work it out.”

So it should. Arion picks the model for the work, and you can still pick one yourself. The controls are all there. They stay out of sight until you ask for them, and every one you don’t have to see is a decision nobody has to make.

The best work happens out of sight

Much of what makes it feel seamless is work you never watch: the branch it brings up to date, the check it reruns, the follow-up it files so nothing drops.

And it shouldn’t wait to be asked. A good colleague tells you about the thing that needs you before you go looking, and stays quiet about the rest. Proactive, not noisy: a tap on the shoulder only when something actually needs you.

Talking is the interface

The most natural interface anyone has is saying what they want. That’s why voice matters so much to me. It’s the shortest path from a thought to a task: walking, between meetings, the moment you remember.

When the voice is good, the screen stops being where you work. It’s where you glance at the result.

Every feature is a design decision

One thread and working from your phone sound like features. They’re design decisions. One thread means there’s nothing to organise, because the agent organises it. Phone-first means everything has to fit a small screen and a few words, which forces out anything that isn’t needed. Each constraint takes something away, and the product gets better for it.

Simple isn’t for beginners

People hear “minimal” and think dumbed down. It’s the opposite. Engineers use it for long, complicated work. People who have never opened a terminal use it for the rest of the company. They like it for the same reason: there’s nothing to learn.

A simple surface is how you carry a lot of complexity underneath without handing any of it to the person.

Simpler every week

Lots of agents promise this. The test is whether you keep reaching for it without thinking, and that gap is made of hundreds of small, invisible things: it remembers what you said yesterday, picks up when a machine dies halfway through, doesn’t make you repeat yourself, and tells you when something’s done instead of making you check.

None of that shows up in a demo. But it compounds. Every week something that used to take a step takes none, and the product gets simpler while it does more.

What it looks like for me

The best thing anyone has said to me about it: a colleague called it the best tool they’ve ever used at work. That’s the compliment I care about most. It isn’t about a feature. It’s about how the thing feels to use.

The tradeoff

Hiding the machinery is easy to overdo. If you can’t see what an agent is doing, you can’t stop it when it’s wrong. So the rule I hold to: hide the machinery, never the decisions. Anything you can’t take back still comes to you, plainly, with a yes or a no.

What’s still hard

Knowing when to speak up and when to stay quiet. Making a default good enough that you never wonder about it, and obvious enough when it’s wrong that you notice. Voice that keeps up in a loud room. Each of those is a place where the interface comes back into view, and the work is making it disappear again.

The less you notice it, the better it’s working.

04

Your agent should never have more access than you.

Agents built like superusers are the risk. Yours should inherit your reach, never widen it.

Read the essay

The fastest way to make an agent useful is to make it powerful. Give it an admin token, a service account that reads every channel, a key to every repository, so it never hits a wall. A lot of agents are built that way. It works brilliantly, right up to the day it doesn’t.

I think the rule should be the opposite, and it fits in one sentence: your agent should never have more access than you.

The superuser problem

An agent with company-wide keys is the most powerful account in the building, and it takes instructions from anyone who can get words in front of it: a message, a page it reads, an email it opens. Give it more reach than the person asking, and you’ve built a way around every permission you already have.

And nobody sees it. The person using it thinks it acts as them. It doesn’t. It acts as something bigger.

Inherit your reach, never widen it

If you can’t open a repository, a channel or a file, neither can it. If your access goes away, its access goes with it. It acts with your authority and never holds your passwords, so if it’s ever confused or compromised, what it carries is small, and never bigger than you.

In a shared room the rule tightens: it knows only what everyone there could know. And it never acts as someone else without their yes.

One set of doors, in one place

“Never more than you” only holds if you can check it. When every tool is wired up differently, with its own sign-in and its own permissions, nobody can answer the simplest question: what can it actually do, and as whom?

So every connection goes through one place, under the same rules, and you can see what’s connected, as whom, and what it can reach. A permission nobody can see is one nobody will ever take back.

Build on the security you already have

A company already decides who gets in: its sign-in, its access rules, the systems its security team watches. An agent shouldn’t build a second world beside that. It should plug into the one that’s there, so the same rules, reviews and alarms cover it too.

The safest agent isn’t the one with the cleverest sandbox. It’s the one your security team already knows how to watch.

Ask less, so it means more

An agent that can’t do more than you doesn’t need to ask about everything. A confirm box on every step is security theatre: by the tenth “Allow?” people tap yes without reading. So it asks rarely and plainly, before money moves, before a message leaves for someone outside, before anything you can’t take back. The question fits on a watch.

What it looked like this week

When it built my site, it hit the walls I would have hit. GitHub wanted me signed in. So did the domain registrar. The mailbox needed my yes before money moved. It didn’t route around any of them. It had no keys of its own, so it came back to me, and I signed in on its screen.

That cost me a minute. It’s also the whole point.

The tradeoff

An agent that can only do what you can will sometimes stop where a superuser would sail through. It waits for a sign-in, or tells you it can’t reach something. That’s friction, and it’s the right kind. Every wall it hits is a permission working.

What’s still hard

Sign-ins that don’t reach a screen in the cloud. Knowing, reliably, which steps can be undone. And making access as easy to take back as it was to give.

Never more than you. Everything else follows from that.
